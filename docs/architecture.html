01What we're building

One sentence, then everything else is plumbing for it.

A photographer finishes a wedding. Instead of dumping 4,000 photos into a Drive folder and letting 200 relatives scroll for an hour, they run the folder through us once. We give them one link. Any guest opens it, takes a selfie, and instantly sees only the photos they're in.

What the photographer experiences

Logs in, points us at a folder, goes to sleep. Wakes up to a link and a page saying "4,102 photos · 68 people found". Sends the link on WhatsApp. Done.

What the aunt experiences

Taps the WhatsApp link on her phone. Sees the full gallery. A button says "Find my photos". She allows camera, takes one selfie. Three seconds later: 37 photos, all of her. Taps download.

The bar

Neither of them ever hears the words embedding, cluster or vector. If either one has to understand anything about how it works, we've failed.

02The decision that shapes everything

Read this section even if you skip the rest. Every other choice follows from it.

A wedding is 2,000–5,000 photos at 5–15 MB each — call it 30–70 GB per event. If we store those, we are dead before customer number one. Storage and bandwidth would cost more than we could plausibly charge, and the cost would recur every month forever.

Core rule

We never store the originals. We read each photo once, keep a tiny web-sized copy plus a mathematical fingerprint of each face, and forget the original. The photographer's own Drive stays the source of truth for full-resolution downloads.

Per weddingIf we stored originalsWhat we actually keep
Photos~50 GB~600 MB of 1000px previews
Faces—~400 MB of small crops
Fingerprints—~100 MB of numbers
Total~50 GB~1.1 GB

A 50× reduction. That is the single reason two people with no funding can build this.

04Box by box, with alternatives

What each piece is, what we picked, what else exists, and why.

Box 1 — Ingest

The job: move 4,000 photos from the photographer to our processor, without us paying to warehouse them.

OptionHow it feels to the photographerReality
Browser folder upload ✓ Drag a folder onto a web page, leave the tab open Dead simple to build, no external approvals. But 50 GB over an Indian home connection takes hours, and a closed laptop lid kills it. Chosen for v1.
Google Drive connect Click "Connect Drive", pick a folder Much better UX, and Google-to-us transfer is fast without using their bandwidth. But it drags in a licensing landmine — see §8.
Small desktop uploader Install a tiny app, point it at a folder Most robust for huge weddings, resumable. More work. Ship later if customers ask.

Box 2 — The Brain

Four steps, and it's less magic than it sounds.

  • Detection — "where are the faces in this photo?" Output: rectangles. A model called SCRFD does it. Fast, and a solved problem.
  • Embedding — the important one. For each face, a model called ArcFace outputs 512 numbers. Think GPS coordinates in a 512-dimensional "face space", built so two photos of the same person land close together and two different people land far apart — regardless of lighting, angle or makeup. Those 512 numbers are about 2 KB. That's the fingerprint, and it's all we keep. Comparing two faces is then just measuring the distance between two points: arithmetic, not AI. That's why search is instant.
  • Quality gate — throw away faces that are too small (under ~50 pixels), too blurry, or turned too far sideways.
  • Clustering — group the ~40,000 fingerprints from one wedding into ~150 piles, one per person.
Do not skip the quality gate

A blurry background head produces a garbage fingerprint, and garbage fingerprints are what make the system show Priya a photo of someone else. One wrong tag destroys more trust than ten missed photos. We are deliberately aggressive here, and we accept missing some real faces as the price.

Clustering is where our actual engineering time goes. Real Indian wedding photos are hostile: heavy makeup, similar outfits, low-light sangeet floors, and a lot of genuinely similar-looking relatives. Expect two or three rounds of tuning, not one.

AlternativeVerdict
AWS Rekognition, Face++, Luxand Roughly ₹0.08–0.80 per image → ₹350–3,500 per wedding in pure API cost. Kills the margin, and we can't tune the threshold ourselves. Only worth it if §8 forces our hand.
face_recognition / dlib Far easier to install, noticeably worse on side angles and low light. Fine to prototype, not to sell.
Training our own model No. Years of work, and unnecessary — this problem is solved.

Box 3 — Store

WhatWherePer wedding
Web previews (~1000px)Cloudflare R2~600 MB
Face cropsCloudflare R2~400 MB
Fingerprints + who-is-in-whatSupabase (Postgres + pgvector)~100 MB
Original photosPhotographer's Drive. Not ours.0

Why Cloudflare R2 and not Amazon S3. R2 charges nothing for bandwidth out. We are an image product — 200 guests browsing a gallery is a lot of bandwidth. On S3 that's a real bill every month, forever, for every wedding we've ever processed. On R2 it's zero. This one choice is worth more to us long-term than any code we'll write.

Why Postgres with pgvector and not a dedicated vector database (Qdrant, Pinecone). At our scale — even 50 weddings — Postgres does vector search perfectly well, and it's the same database already holding users, events and payments. One service instead of two. We add Qdrant when we outgrow Postgres, which would be a very nice problem to have.

Box 4 — Serve

  • Website — Next.js. One codebase for both the photographer's dashboard and the guest gallery.
  • Hosting — Cloudflare Pages. Its free tier permits commercial use; Vercel's free tier does not (§8).
  • Selfie search API — a small always-on server that takes a selfie, produces a fingerprint, and asks Postgres "which faces are near this point?"
  • Heavy processing — our own desktop, for now. The machine wakes up, asks "any weddings to process?", does the work, uploads results, sleeps. Cost ₹0, no cold starts, no free-tier limits, no Docker on day one. Written as a queue puller so moving it to a rented GPU later is a config change, not a rewrite.

Box 5 — Money

Razorpay, charged per event rather than monthly. Reasoning in §7.

05The data model

Six tables. That's genuinely all of it.

TableOne row is…Holds
photographersa customerWho's paying
eventsone weddingName, date, share link, processing status
photosone photoFilename, preview location, pointer back to the original
facesone detected faceWhich photo, where in it, the 512 numbers, a quality score
peopleone cluster"Person 14", later renamed "Priya"; an average fingerprint for the group
searchesone selfie searchUsage proof and debugging when someone complains

The core relationship: a photo has many faces; each face belongs to one person. "Show me Priya's photos" is one ordinary database query — nothing clever required.

06Follow one photo, and one guest

The abstract stuff made concrete.

One photo, from upload to gallery

  1. IMG_4821.jpg, 12 MB, arrives at the worker.
  2. Shrunk to 1000px wide, uploaded to R2. 12 MB → 140 KB · original discarded
  3. Detector finds 6 faces. Two are blurry background heads. quality gate → 4 survive
  4. Each of the 4 is cropped and uploaded, then turned into 512 numbers.
  5. Four rows written to faces, one to photos.
  6. The original 12 MB file is gone from our side — still safe in the photographer's Drive. net: 12 MB in, ~180 KB kept

One guest, from selfie to results

  1. Aunt taps "Find my photos" and takes a selfie.
  2. Selfie goes to our API, becomes 512 numbers, and is deleted immediately. we never store guest selfies — and we say so on the page
  3. Postgres: "every face within distance X of this point, in this event." ~80 milliseconds
  4. We pull those photos' previews from R2 and show them.
  5. "Download originals" links straight through to the photographer's Drive.
Why step 2 matters commercially

Telling guests plainly that their selfie is discarded the moment it's converted is the difference between this feeling clever and feeling creepy. It's also most of our compliance story. It goes on the page in plain language, not buried in a privacy policy.

* Oracle's free ARM tier is genuinely free and generously sized, but ARM capacity is frequently unavailable in Indian regions and idle accounts have been reclaimed in the past. Treat it as a nice-to-have with a ~₹350/mo VPS as the real plan.

Honest total

For the first 5–10 weddings: between ₹0 and about ₹2,000 a month.

The only thing that pushes us off the free tiers is old weddings we're still storing. Which hands us a natural policy: galleries stay live for 90 days, then previews are deleted — the originals were never ours anyway. That caps storage permanently and doubles as our data-retention story for §8.

What we charge

Photographers work event to event, not monthly. A subscription asks them to pay in the months they shoot nothing, which is the fastest route to a cancellation. So: price per event, tiered by photo count — something like ₹1,500 / ₹3,000 / ₹5,000. Treat those numbers as a hypothesis to test on the first five photographers, not a decision we've made.

The real risk in this business

The technology is not the risk — face recognition plus clustering is a solved problem. The risk is that the photographer feels only mild pain here, while the guest feels the real pain — and guests don't buy software. So which is it: the photographer buys it as a differentiator to win bookings, or the couple buys it as an add-on? Five conversations answers this. Have them before we've built three months of product.

08Landmines

Invisible until they're suddenly a wall. On the table now, not in month four.

1 — Model licensing

The InsightFace code is open, but the pretrained model weights — the actually valuable part — are licensed for non-commercial research use only, as far as we currently understand it. Fine for building, testing and demoing. Not fine the day someone pays us.

Options: read the current licence text ourselves (it may have changed); switch to a permissively-licensed model with somewhat lower accuracy; or price a commercial API into the unit economics. Does not block the next two months — but must be settled before invoice number one.

2 — Google Drive permissions

The drive.readonly permission is a restricted scope. Using it in a public app requires an annual third-party security assessment costing real money — thousands of dollars a year.

The way around it is Google's file Picker with the narrower drive.file permission, where the photographer explicitly selects the folder and we only ever get access to that. It's a different code path, so it matters that we design for it rather than retrofit. Deferred for now — v1 uses browser upload, which sidesteps the whole thing.

3 — Biometric data law

Face fingerprints are biometric personal data under India's DPDP Act. Practically that means: a consent screen for guests, the 90-day deletion policy from §7, a "delete me" button, and clarity in the photographer contract that they are the data fiduciary and we are the processor.

An hour with a lawyer before the first paid event. The rules have been shifting, so don't trust anything written a year ago — including this document.

4 — Minor, but real

Vercel's free tier prohibits commercial use. Hence Cloudflare Pages. Cheap to get right now, annoying to discover later.

The consequence of CPU-only

On a CPU, fingerprinting a full wedding takes roughly one to two hours. Fine overnight. Completely unworkable when you want to try ten clustering variations in an afternoon. So the pipeline is split in two, permanently:

STAGE 1 — expensive, runs ONCE per wedding, cached to disk
  photos → detect faces → quality gate → 512-number fingerprints → faces.parquet

STAGE 2 — cheap, re-runs in seconds, unlimited times
  faces.parquet → cluster → evaluate → report

Stage 1 runs overnight. After that every clustering experiment reads the cached file and finishes in about five seconds — a hundred tuning iterations a day on a laptop. This one split is what makes "no GPU" a non-issue, and it's the right design anyway: a GPU later just makes stage 1 faster without changing anything else.

10Build order

Weeks 1–2 have no user interface on purpose.

WhenWhat"Done" means
Week 1–2Local scripts only. Folder in → face clusters out. No website at all. On a real wedding, ≥90% of one person's faces land in a single pile, with near-zero wrong faces mixed in.
Week 3–4Shareable gallery link + selfie search. Ugly is fine. We can WhatsApp a link to Adarsh's mother and she finds her photos unaided.
Month 2Photographer login, upload flow, downloads, consent + deletion. A photographer can use it without either of us in the room.
Month 3Five photographers, free, in exchange for their data and brutal feedback. Payments last. We know who pays, and how much.

If the clustering doesn't work, a beautiful gallery is worthless. And if it does work, everything after it is ordinary web development. So we find out first.

The first three things that get written

  1. A script, no UI. Point it at a folder on disk. It walks every image, detects faces, discards the bad ones, writes fingerprints to a cache file, and saves face crops so we can see what it found.
  2. The clustering script. Reads the cache, groups the faces, and produces an HTML page — "Person 1 — 84 photos" with a thumbnail grid under each. We scroll it and count the mistakes by eye. That page is our accuracy meter, and we'll be staring at it for two weeks.
  3. Tune against real photos until the piles are clean.

No database, no cloud, no login, no upload until week 3.

11Who does what

Claude writes every line of code. That frees the two of us for what it can't do.

WhoOwns
AdarshProduct calls, the five photographer conversations, pricing — and the one job that genuinely can't be outsourced: getting real wedding photo sets to test on.
DeveshRuns the processing machine, and does the human accuracy testing: take 200 photos, check by eye how many the system got right and wrong. Tedious, essential, and the only honest measure of whether this works.
ClaudeAll code, all infrastructure, all tuning.
The current bottleneck

Clustering quality is the product, and it cannot be tuned against imaginary photos. We need roughly one real wedding, or ~500 photos of a group of 20-ish recurring people, before any tuning work means anything. Everything before that can be built blind — but not for long.

Three sources, in order of value:

  1. Ask a photographer friend for one wedding folder now. Offer free processing forever in exchange. This is simultaneously our test data and our first sales conversation — we'll learn whether they even feel the pain, which is the real question from §7.
  2. Our own phone galleries. Family functions, group shots, the same people across years and lighting conditions. Genuinely useful — same problem shape, smaller.
  3. Public face datasets — only useful for confirming the fingerprinting works at all. They're cropped celebrity headshots, nothing like a sangeet floor. Don't tune on them.

12Open questions for Devesh

Reply by number — these are the things that change what gets written first.

Q1 · Hardware

Do you have, or can you get access to, any machine with an NVIDIA graphics card?

Not a blocker — but it turns a two-hour processing run into fifteen minutes, which matters a lot in the weeks where we're re-running things daily.

Q2 · Test photos

How many photos can you assemble this week, and do you know any wedding photographer even loosely?

Even a few hundred family-function photos lets us verify the pipeline end to end while we chase a real wedding folder.

Q3 · Who pays

Read §7's risk box. Your instinct: does the photographer buy this, or the couple?

We should each write an answer down before we start asking people, so we can tell later whether we were right or just persuaded ourselves.

Q4 · Anything here you'd build differently

Specifically §4 (R2 vs S3, Postgres vs a vector database) and §10's "no UI for two weeks".

The second one is the one people usually argue with. Now is the cheap time to argue.

Q5 · Time

Realistically, how many hours a week?

Changes whether the build order above is four weeks or ten. Better to know now and plan honestly than to discover it in month two.
